The Challenge

A blast tool being asked to become a compliance system

Email Blast did one thing: send a message to a list. It was never built to know whether the message had been personalized beyond what was approved, whether the recipient list was still accurate by the time it went out, or what a firm would show a regulator if asked to prove what was sent and why. Financial services customers were increasingly using it anyway, because it was the tool they had — which meant every gap in it became a compliance exposure someone eventually had to explain.

The harder problem was that "bulk email" was never really one workflow. A seller sending a single personalized message before a meeting, a marketer running a campaign that re-syncs its recipient list every week, a birthday email triggered automatically by a CRM event nobody is watching in the moment, and a rep sending from an iPad between meetings are four completely different operating conditions. Design them as four unrelated features and the product fragments — different compliance behavior, different recipient handling, different vocabulary for the same underlying status. Design them as one workflow and you lose the speed each context actually needs.

And underneath all of it sat the constraint that made this a design problem rather than a build problem: personalization is the entire point of the product, and personalization is exactly what triggers compliance review. A tool that makes editing easy and a tool that keeps every edit governed are, by default, in tension with each other.

My Role

Designing on top of governance, not around it

Bulk Email is explicitly an anchor experience for Seismic's governance service and Compliance Workflow 2.0 — one of the first products meant to build directly on that platform rather than invent its own approval logic. That set the terms of the role. My job was not to design a review workflow from scratch; it was to answer, for email specifically, the questions the platform leaves to each product: what counts as a content object, what edit is significant enough to require re-review, what a draft means before it's ever sent, and what "done" looks like for a message that might get resent to a refreshed list next month.

The recurring decision throughout was how much of "bulk email" should be one system and how much should flex per workflow. I pushed hard for a single status model — the same ten states whether the message is a one-time blast or the fortieth run of a recurring campaign — because a support team, a compliance reviewer, and a supervisor should not have to learn a different vocabulary for every entry point into the same product. Where I did not push for uniformity was configuration: what a seller is allowed to edit on a recurring campaign is an administrator decision made per campaign, not a global setting, because no two firms run outreach the same way.

Mobile forced the sharpest trade-off. A seller sending from a tablet between meetings needs nearly everything a desktop sender needs — template selection, personalization, recipient validation, compliance status, send confirmation — just not all of it at once. The design direction I've been carrying forward is to keep every safeguard and remove configuration, not the other way around: a simplified surface with the same guardrails, not a lighter-weight product that happens to run on a phone.

The Core Insight

The same question, asked at five different speeds

Every workflow in Bulk Email is answering one question: is this specific, personalized message safe to send to this specific recipient, right now? What changes between workflows is not the question but the clock. A seller wants that answer in seconds. A recurring campaign has to answer it fresh every time its recipient list re-syncs. An event-triggered email has to answer it without a human in the loop at the moment of send. A bulk welcome email has to answer it for up to 150 recipients at once, with a clear account of who succeeded and who didn't.

Once I treated speed as the variable and the underlying question as constant, the same three-part shape held everywhere, just recomposed differently for each context.

01
Personalize
Content and variables resolved within clearly defined permissions — locked sections shown as locked, not merely unavailable, so the user understands the boundary rather than guessing at it.
02
Validate
Recipients and content checked before send — invalid addresses, duplicates, and unresolved variables surfaced clearly, with an explicit distinction between validating a list now and validating it again at send time.
03
Govern
Messages routed through compliance review when personalization requires it, with status, ownership, and audit history preserved identically regardless of which of the five workflows produced the message.
Where the Craft Went

The distinctions users never see, until they get them wrong

Most of the difficult decisions on Bulk Email don't render as a screen. Three examples of the kind of thing that took the most time to get right.

Locked is not the same as unavailable. A field a seller can't edit because an administrator locked it for compliance is a completely different situation from a field that's simply missing data — but rendered identically, both just look broken. Locked content has to look locked, with a reason attached, or users spend their time reporting bugs that are actually policy.

Validating now is not the same as validating at send time. A recipient list checked at 9am and sent at 2pm has had five hours to go stale — someone unsubscribed, a record changed, a sync ran. Treating an earlier validation as still true at send time is how firms end up explaining an email that shouldn't have gone out. The two checks have to be visibly separate events, not one status the user assumes still holds.

A change to one send is not the same as a change to all future sends. On a recurring campaign, editing today's message, editing the standard template, and editing the campaign's cadence are three different blast radii. Collapsing them into a single "save" is the fastest way to have a seller accidentally change a message a hundred other people are relying on for next month.

Building the Pattern Once

Five workflows, one set of parts

Rather than design each workflow as its own product, I've been building a shared set of patterns that each one composes differently — the same way the governance service itself separates a common workflow engine from what's genuinely local to each surface.

One status model — Draft through Delivered — reused across one-time blasts, recurring campaigns, and event-triggered sends, instead of a bespoke status set per workflow
A single pre-send validation experience for recipients and content, carried through to a reduced but equally safeguarded mobile version
A reviewer experience that renders the email exactly as the recipient will see it, with variables resolved rather than shown as raw tokens
Bulk Welcome Email built on the same recipient-validation and audit patterns as customer-facing sends, rather than shipped as a one-off admin utility
Compliance, Inherited and Extended

What the reviewer needs that a generic queue doesn't have

Because Bulk Email sits on the governance platform, the reviewer gets the same queue, the same claim-and-decide actions, and the same audit trail as every other compliance surface at Seismic. What it needed on top of that was specific to email: the fully rendered message as the recipient will actually see it, HTML and formatting included, rather than a source view; recipient-specific values resolved in context rather than displayed as tokens; and enough framing around sender, campaign, and requested send time that a reviewer isn't reconstructing context the system already has.

Supervisors needed a different view of the same data — pending approvals across the firm regardless of who they're assigned to, filterable by sender, campaign, and age, so a bottleneck shows up as a queue getting old rather than as a complaint. Governance without visibility just relocates the honor system one level up.

Where It Stands

Sequenced, not simultaneous

The roadmap spreads this out deliberately rather than trying to ship five workflows at once. Near-term work is establishing the foundations — global variables, DSR sharing, IP pooling, and explicit state transitions for Bulk Email itself. Recurring delivery, dynamic list refresh, and a redesigned template selection experience follow. Mobile Bulk Email and Bulk Welcome Email land later, once the desktop patterns they extend are settled. I'm treating that sequencing as roadmap themes to design toward, not commitments to hold fixed — the order is likely to shift as dependencies on the governance platform and the email service become clearer.

The open question I haven't settled is what happens when a recipient list is only partially valid. Blocking the entire send over a handful of bad addresses punishes the sender for data they don't control. Silently dropping invalid recipients and sending anyway removes a decision the sender should probably get to make. My current direction is to make the valid and invalid counts explicit and require an active choice — send to the valid subset, fix the list, or cancel — rather than picking a default on the user's behalf. Whether that's the right amount of friction for a seller trying to send before a meeting is something I want usability testing to answer, not something to assume from a desk.

What I'm Taking From It

Designing one product that doesn't feel like five

The temptation on a project like this is to let each workflow become its own small design effort — a one-time-send screen, a campaign builder, a mobile app, each solved on its own terms. What's held the work together instead is designing the shared question first — is this message safe to send to this recipient right now — and only then asking what each context needs to answer it at its own speed.

That's the same discipline the governance platform itself is built on, applied one level down. It's also the reason I think Bulk Email will hold up as the roadmap adds workflows I haven't designed yet: the parts are shared, so the fifth workflow costs less than the first one did.
