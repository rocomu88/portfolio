Program Leadership · Platform Design · Regulated Workflows

One compliance model,
many experiences

Compliance had been built as a feature, over and over, inside one product area after another. The result was a platform where every surface handled approval differently and none of them handled it well enough for a regulated firm to trust. I led the design program that replaced that pattern with a single governance service — and, more importantly, built the design system and the embedded evangelist model that let squads across the org apply it without me in the room.

The Challenge

The honor system, at enterprise scale

Financial services firms operate under rules that leave no room for interpretation. FINRA and SEC requirements mean every piece of client-facing material has to be governed by policy, supervised before it goes out, and archived in a form regulators can retrieve. Firms that get this wrong are fined.

Here is what the workflow actually looked like for an advisor who wanted to personalize a document. Customize it in the platform. Download it. Upload it into a separate third-party review tool. Wait. The reviewer downloads it from that tool, marks it up, uploads it back. The advisor is notified in the third-party tool, returns to the platform to make the edit, downloads again, re-uploads again. The reviewer downloads again. Eventually something gets approved, and the advisor downloads it one final time to upload it back where it started.

And at the end of all of that, nothing stopped the advisor from editing the approved version anyway. Firms had no mechanism to enforce their own policy. They were relying on the honor system — which is why most of them simply turned features off instead.

That was the commercial problem underneath the design problem. Compliance gaps were not a satisfaction issue; they were blocking deals outright and capping what existing customers were willing to switch on.

My Role as Design Leader

Leading a program I could not personally design

This is the distinction that defined the work for me. On earlier initiatives I could own the experience end to end myself. Here the surface area was four product areas owned by different squads, an admin configuration layer, a reviewer experience that had to work identically everywhere, and archival integrations with external vendors. No single designer could hold that, and trying would have made me the bottleneck on a program with a hard October deadline.

So I led it as a program rather than a project. I set the design strategy and the compliance-by-design vision, ran the alignment with my engineering and product counterparts, structured design ownership and tracking across squads, and coordinated with the other design managers whose teams would carry pieces of the work. I also led the design work for archival of engagements directly, because staying hands-on in one area kept my judgment on the rest of it honest.

The pivotal moment was a three-day onsite I helped drive to convert an open-ended program into decisions. We went in with two questions: how do we build compliance as a platform rather than a per-app feature, and what exactly ships in October. We came out with five locked decisions, including the architecture, the archival vendor, the surface scope, and a deliberate simplification — one workflow per application at MVP instead of context-conditional multi-workflow. That last one was the call that made an October date real.

A quieter decision I would defend just as hard: the engine ships on for every customer, with an empty configuration that behaves exactly like today. Nothing gates until a firm configures a rule. Replacing the approval layer under a platform that enterprises run their businesses on is the kind of change that generates incidents, and the only version of it I was willing to sign off on was one where the default state is indistinguishable from the status quo. Ambition in the model, conservatism in the rollout.

The Core Insight

Separating the model from the experience

The insight that unlocked the program was that compliance is one model expressed as many experiences. Submission, review, and decision are the same conceptual sequence whether the content is a bulk email, a digital sales room, a shared link, or a working document. What differs is the surface, not the semantics.

That let us split responsibility cleanly. The governance service owns the workflow engine, the compliance state model, the reviewer experience, and the audit and archival hooks. Application teams define only what is genuinely local to them: what event triggers review, what counts as their content object, what a draft state means, and when the work is done.

We named the interface between those two things the compliance contract. It sounds like an engineering artifact, but it was really a design and alignment tool: a shared set of questions every team had to answer the same way. What is a content object, and what is a version? What are the valid compliance states and how do they transition? What modification is significant enough to trigger re-review? Getting teams to answer those consistently was most of the actual work.

The single most clarifying decision we made was pulling two things apart that everyone had been treating as one. Whether a piece of content is approved and where a review has got to are separate facts. A document can be approved while a new submission on it is in review. A submission can be rejected without the previously approved version losing its approval. Collapsing those into one status is what had produced years of almost-right behavior across the platform, so we tracked them on two independent axes and made it a rule that neither one may be inferred from the other.

From there the model settled into nine canonical states — and this is where the thesis became literal rather than rhetorical. The canonical state never changes; only its label does. One state reads as Submitted to the person who sent it, Unclaimed to a reviewer scanning a queue for work, and Unassigned to an admin deciding who should take it. Same fact, three vocabularies, because those are three genuinely different jobs. One model, many experiences, expressed at the level of a single word.

Where the Craft Went

The distinctions that don't look like design work

Most of the difficulty on this program lived in decisions that never render as a screen. Three examples of the kind of thing we spent real time on.

Rejected and needs-changes are not the same verdict. A reviewer asking for an edit is a soft outcome — the submitter revises and continues in the same session, and the work keeps its history. Rejection is terminal: the submission is over, and continuing means starting a new one. We required a reason on rejection for that reason. The two carry different consequences for the person on the other end, so flattening them into one negative state would have been a kindness to the system and a cruelty to the user.

Revoking an approval cannot erase it. If something was approved and later pulled back, the firm still needs to prove it was legitimately approved at the moment it went out. So revoke returns the item to review without rewriting the past: approvals from earlier steps stand, later steps restart, and the record shows both the approval and the reversal. Designing the undo was harder than designing the action.

Archive, audit, and activity are three different things. They get conflated constantly. The archive is an external regulatory record of what a recipient saw and who authorized it. The audit trail is internal traceability. Activity is the in-product history a user reads to understand what happened to their work. Different audiences, retention rules, and levels of detail — and only one of them is a user interface.

Scaling the Team

Compliance-by-design, taught rather than policed

A single compliance model only holds if the designers building on it understand it. The failure mode I wanted to avoid was becoming a review gate — every squad's compliance work routed through me for approval, which would have been slower than the workflow we were replacing.

Instead I established the compliance-by-design evangelist program. Seven designers embedded across engagements, learning, and content, each responsible for applying compliance patterns inside their own squad. Alongside it, a shared compliance design system covering the recurring pieces — compliance indicators, review queues, audit logs, approval states — so an evangelist reaches for a pattern instead of inventing one.

The bet was that distributing the knowledge would scale better than centralizing the decisions. It also gave seven designers exposure to regulated design work and a visible role in a company-priority program, which was the part I cared about most.

Reviewer Experience

Telling the reviewer what actually changed

Reviewers are the constraint in every compliance system. They are a small team, they are legally accountable, and the volume they face is the reason firms restrict personalization in the first place. Speeding up review without weakening it was the highest-value problem on the program.

The approach we took was to reduce what a reviewer has to reconstruct. When a submission arrives, our AI assistant summarizes the delta against the previously approved version in plain language — what was added, what was rewritten, and specifically which claims need legal confirmation. The reviewer can then toggle between the submitted version, the previous version, or both, with changes highlighted.

The design principle held from the rest of the program: the assistant narrows attention, the reviewer makes the decision, and the audit trail records the human action. In a regulated workflow, an AI that decides is a liability. An AI that tells you where to look is leverage.

Where It Stands

Building toward the October release

The program is in build against a staged milestone plan, with the MVP scoped to four surfaces and each one required to be sellable, in pilot, or demo-ready at our annual customer conference. Archival moved into pilot ahead of the broader release, and the approval workflow and external ad-review integration are tracking to the same date.

What I would point to as the real outcome, before any of the launch metrics exist, is that compliance stopped being something each product area solved on its own. That change is organizational as much as architectural, and it is the part that will still be true in two years.

The longer arc is bigger than compliance. The service was deliberately built as a generic, domain-agnostic workflow engine with no compliance logic baked into it, which means the same machinery can eventually absorb the platform's ordinary content publishing workflow. We scoped the first release to compliance because it was the urgent problem, not because it was the boundary of the idea.

There are still things we have not settled. The sharpest is a genuine tension in the model: when no rule applies to a piece of content, should the system treat it as approved or as ungoverned? Defaulting to approved keeps the product usable and matches how everything behaves today. Defaulting to ungoverned is the safer regulatory posture and the honest answer for a surface nobody has instrumented yet. Both positions are defensible, both are written into our requirements, and reconciling them is on me.

The same submissions model rendered for two different roles — the admin configures the workflow; the submitter sees only their own work and no decision actions.

What I Took From It

The leverage moved

Earlier in my career the way to increase my impact was to design more, better, faster. On this program the highest-leverage things I did were choosing what not to build for the first release, defining the contract that let teams work in parallel, and putting seven designers in a position to carry compliance work I would otherwise have had to touch myself.

None of those show up as a screen in a portfolio. All of them are the reason four product surfaces are converging on one model instead of drifting into four more versions of the problem we started with.
