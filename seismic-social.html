Work › Seismic Social
Design Leadership · Compliance-by-Design · Financial Services

From acquired tool,
rebuilt into the platform

Seismic Social started as an acquired, standalone tool that advisors had to leave the platform to use — social selling in one place, compliance in another, everything else Seismic offered somewhere else entirely. I led design for modernizing it into an embedded, compliance-by-design experience built directly into the product advisors already work in. We validated the rebuild with a cohort of design-partner firms, shipped the pilot on schedule, and it earned a company MVP award. The work now continues as a structured, multi-year migration for the rest of the customer base.

The Challenge

A tool that lived outside the product advisors already used

The product Seismic had acquired did what it was built to do — social content curation, publishing, and compliance review for financial advisors. What it didn't do was live inside Seismic. An advisor working in Seismic for everything else had to leave the platform entirely to draft a post, check whether it needed compliance review, or see whether it had gone out. Every one of those context switches was a place a regulated workflow could quietly fall out of view.

The roadmap direction that came out of that problem was, on paper, unglamorous: port the experience into the product advisors were already in, rather than keep improving it as its own destination. That framing mattered more than it sounds. It meant the design problem wasn't "make this tool better" — it was "make this capability disappear into a product it was never built for," which is a much harder brief than shipping new features in isolation.

The legacy experience: a capable content feed, but a destination of its own — every share, edit, and review meant leaving the product advisors actually worked in.

My Role as Design Leader

Designing for five personas who don't trust each other's tools

I led design from early prototype through the pilot that shipped. The product had to work for a seller who wants to post in under a minute, a curator assembling approved content at scale, an administrator configuring rules for an entire firm, a compliance reviewer who is legally accountable for what goes out, and a migration stakeholder trying to move dozens of existing customers off the legacy tool without breaking their workflows. Five genuinely different jobs, one product.

The recurring judgment call was how much of that difference to design for explicitly. Early internal testing surfaced a real lesson here: a single unified interface for admins and end users looked efficient on paper, but it didn't match how regulated firms actually operate — many of them run strictly segregated roles, and a shared surface made that separation harder to see, not easier. That's not a mistake I'd call a failure so much as a case for building in the option to separate roles cleanly rather than assuming convergence was free.

The same underlying content, rendered for two different jobs — the advisor sees what they can share; the curator sees what they own, and the controls to govern it.

I also led the design decisions on where AI assistance fit into a regulated posting workflow. Advisors can get help drafting a post; nothing about that draft is compliant until a human — or a defined review workflow — says so. Keeping that distinction visible in the interface, rather than letting an AI-assisted draft imply it had been vetted, was a non-negotiable design constraint I held throughout the work.

The Aura-assisted drafting flow. Each step keeps the draft in the author's hands — generation produces a suggestion, never a post that is cleared to send.

The Core Insight

Make the compliant path the fastest one

Compliant social selling tools are usually sold as a tradeoff: move fast, or stay governed — pick one. The design bet underneath this whole rebuild was that the tradeoff is a design failure, not a fact of regulated work. If the compliant path is also the fastest path, sellers stop needing a reason to take it.

That thesis showed up as a small set of concrete rules I held across every workflow in the product.

01
Visible at the point of action
Channel rules and review requirements show up before a seller invests effort in a post, not after — so nobody drafts something they can't send.
02
Delegated, not blurred
Posting on someone's behalf and recommending content to them are different actions with different accountability — the product never collapses them into one permission.
03
Assisted, not authorized
AI-drafted content is labeled as a suggestion a human still owns — the interface never implies a generated post has already been cleared.
Where the Craft Went

The lessons that only showed up in testing

Some of the most useful decisions on this program came from things that didn't work the first time.

A unified interface is not the same as a simpler one. Designing one shared surface for admins and end users looked like it would reduce complexity. In practice, firms that segregate those roles for compliance reasons found the shared surface harder to reason about, not easier — a reminder that "fewer screens" and "less complexity" are not the same design goal.

Configured is not the same as enabled. When AI drafting was turned on for a firm but the organization's style and content rules hadn't been configured yet, the result looked broken rather than incomplete. The fix wasn't a better error message — it was treating "on but unconfigured" as its own state the interface had to explain, not a variant of "working" or "broken."

Compliant is not a property of the content — it's a property of the workflow. A post isn't "compliant" or "non-compliant" in the abstract; it's compliant against a specific firm's rules, at a specific point in review. Naming that precisely, rather than labeling anything a generic "compliant" badge, was the difference between a UI that builds trust with a compliance reviewer and one that quietly asks them to take our word for it.

Beyond the Pilot

Designing for a migration, not just a launch

Shipping the pilot with our design partners was the easier half of the problem. The product also has to absorb every customer still running the legacy tool, and those customers range from simple, single-network setups to firms with custom integrations and years of configuration behind them. That isn't a single migration; it's dozens of them, each with its own risk profile.

The design contribution here was less about screens and more about the artifacts that make a migration legible to the people running it: a legacy-to-new-platform terminology dictionary so support and services teams could translate configuration concepts consistently, and configuration-mapping patterns that let a customer's existing rules carry over instead of being rebuilt from scratch. Migration UX that isn't designed deliberately becomes a support burden six months after launch — designing it up front was cheaper than discovering that later.

Where It Stands

Shipped, and now scaling

The pilot is done: it shipped on schedule with our design-partner cohort and earned the team a company MVP award. What's active now is the harder, less visible part — moving the rest of the customer base off the legacy product on a structured, cohort-based timeline, sequenced by complexity rather than all at once.

The open tension I haven't fully resolved is how much of that migration should be self-serve versus white-glove. Simple customers can move through a short, mostly self-directed process. The most complex ones — custom integrations, years of firm-specific configuration — genuinely can't move safely without significant hands-on support, and no amount of better onboarding design changes that. Deciding which customers get which path, and being honest that some of them aren't ready yet, is a harder call than it looks from a roadmap slide.

What I Took From It

Shipping is a milestone, not the finish line

The pilot and the MVP award are the part of this work I can point to as done. But the design decisions that will matter most — whether a unified interface actually served every persona, whether the migration path holds up for the customer with the strangest legacy configuration — are still being tested against reality as more of the customer base moves over.

That's the shift I'd point to from earlier in my career: the milestone that used to feel like the finish line — the launch, the award — is now the point where the harder design problem starts.
